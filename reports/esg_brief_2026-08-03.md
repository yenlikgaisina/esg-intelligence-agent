## 1. Regulation Watch

*   **EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) - Live Financial Obligation:**
    *   **What it is:** The EU's CBAM became a live financial obligation on January 1, 2026, imposing carbon costs on imports of carbon-intensive goods, including iron, steel, cement, and aluminum, equivalent to the carbon price paid by EU producers.
    *   **Who it affects:** Directly impacts Kazakhstan's aluminum, iron, and steel exporters to the EU. The EU accounted for 54% of Kazakhstan's aluminum exports.
    *   **Effective date:** January 1, 2026 (financial obligation).
    *   **Business implication:** Companies failing to monitor and report actual emissions risk being charged based on significantly higher EU default values, including additional markups of 10% in 2026, rising to 30% by 2028. This could lead to CBAM costs exceeding €108 million (US$125 million) for iron and steel, potentially 123% of some export values. Properly monitored and reported emissions could halve this liability to around €57 million (US$66 million) annually. Kazakhstan's low domestic carbon price (~€0.40/ton vs. EU's ~€80/ton) means exporters effectively pay the full EU carbon price. (Source: The Astana Times, June 4, 2026).

*   **Kazakhstan Construction Code 2026 Enacted:**
    *   **What it is:** The new Construction Code, signed January 9, 2026, replaces previous laws and brings significant changes to construction activities within Kazakhstan.
    *   **Who it affects:** All construction companies, contractors, and project managers operating in Kazakhstan.
    *   **Effective date:** July 1, 2026.
    *   **Business implication:** Key changes include new internal material consumption standards and actual purchase price recording for construction costs. Businesses must update accounting policies and cost control procedures to comply with these new regulations. (Source: JDSupra, 2026).

For a hypothetical building using approximately 100 tonnes of structural steel, 800 m³ of concrete, and 15 tonnes of rebar, the embodied carbon hotspots would primarily be the structural steel (due to high carbon intensity per kg) and the concrete (due to its high volume and cement content).

**Hotspots:**
*   **Structural Steel:** Conventional BOF steel is highly carbon-intensive. This is a primary target for CBAM if exported to the EU or for domestic projects seeking lower carbon footprints.
*   **Concrete:** The cement content in concrete is a major contributor to embodied carbon.

## Embodied Carbon Trends

The rules for steel and concrete are fundamentally changing in 2026:
*   **Procurement Shifts:** Demand for greener materials is increasingly shaping EU and UK procurement, moving from encouragement to expectation. Carbon data is now essential, not optional, with procurement frameworks rewarding transparency and low-emissions performance.
*   **Carbon Disclosure as Baseline:** Carbon accounting is becoming a core business requirement for market competitiveness. Alignment of standards (e.g., ResponsibleSteel) is influencing procurement criteria and access to finance. Scrutiny is intensifying on the credibility and governance of accounting approaches, with attention on whether claims are linked to actual emissions reductions.
*   **Performance-Based Concrete Standards:** The focus is shifting from rigid ingredient lists to performance-based standards. Digital tools and AI-enabled mix optimization are accelerating the adoption of low-carbon concrete by prioritizing strength, durability, and performance, opening doors for innovative new blends and materials.
*   **Green Hydrogen Steel Scaling:** Green hydrogen-based Direct Reduced Iron (DRI) steel production is moving beyond pilot projects towards commercialization, offering a credible pathway to near-zero primary steel. New supply agreements and continued progress signal growing commercial confidence.
*   **Investor Scrutiny:** Private developers, building owners, and corporate builders are integrating embodied carbon into material choices, driven by investor demands and the need to future-proof assets. Data centers are leading this trend, with their scale and long horizons driving early engagement with low-carbon concrete suppliers. (Source: The Climate Group, January 28, 2026).

## Recommended Actions

1.  **Prioritize Emissions Monitoring and Reporting for CBAM:** Immediately implement robust systems for measuring, calculating, and verifying actual carbon emissions for all aluminum, iron, and steel products destined for the EU. This is the single most effective way to halve CBAM liabilities and avoid punitive default charges.
2.  **Integrate Kazakhstan's New Construction Code:** Review and update internal accounting policies, material procurement standards, and cost control procedures to align with the new Construction Code effective July 1, 2026. Ensure all project teams are aware of the new material consumption and costing requirements.
3.  **Explore Low-Carbon Material Sourcing:** Actively seek suppliers of lower-carbon structural steel (e.g., EAF with high recycled content) and concrete (e.g., high SCM content). Begin requesting Environmental Product Declarations (EPDs) for key materials to gather verifiable carbon data, which will be crucial for CBAM compliance, green building certifications, and future-proofing.
4.  **Assess Supply Chain Carbon Footprint:** For companies involved in export to the EU or with European supply chains, begin mapping and assessing the embodied carbon footprint of key construction materials and components. This proactive step will identify hotspots and opportunities for decarbonization ahead of stricter regulations or client demands.
5.  **Advocate for Domestic Carbon Pricing Alignment:** While a long-term goal, supporting the government's efforts to strengthen Kazakhstan's domestic emissions trading system could eventually reduce CBAM-related costs for exporters by allowing domestic carbon payments to be offset against EU charges.
